Chinese Wedding Customs: A Practical Guide

VowToDo · 27 July 2026 · 9 min

Chinese wedding customs look like a maze of dates and gifts, especially when both families have opinions. Here is what each one is for, roughly when it happens, and how Singapore couples adapt it. Dialect groups differ (Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese, Hainanese, Hakka). Your elders are the authority for your family. This is the map, not the ruling.

Auspicious dates

Many families check the Tong Shu, or ask someone who reads dates, before fixing the wedding, the Guo Da Li and the An Chuang. The couple’s birth dates go into that. Couples who do not believe in it still often pick an “approved” date. It costs nothing, and it matters to grandparents. If you are going to do it, do it before you pay a ballroom deposit.

Guo Da Li (过大礼)

Usually two to four weeks before the wedding. The groom’s family delivers betrothal gifts to the bride’s family: the pin jin (a bride price the families have already agreed), oranges in even numbers, cakes and sweets, and items that depend on dialect. The bride’s family returns a portion, hui li, so it reads as a shared blessing rather than a purchase. Wedding shops in Chinatown sell dialect-specific sets if you do not want to assemble one.

Si Dian Jin (四点金)

Traditionally from the groom’s mother to the bride: four pieces of gold (necklace, bangle, ring, earrings), given at Guo Da Li or at the tea ceremony. The meaning is straightforward. She will be looked after.

An Chuang (安床)

On an auspicious date before the wedding, the matrimonial bed is made with new red bedding. People scatter red dates, longans and lotus seeds, a pun on 早生贵子, having children soon. A child bouncing on the bed is the classic touch. The bed is then not slept in alone until the wedding night.

Gatecrashing (闯门)

On the morning, the groom and his brothers come to fetch the bride. The sisters block the door with games: something sour, sweet, bitter, spicy, a dare, a few push-ups, until he pays an ang bao toll. It runs long. It is also where the best photographs of the day usually are. Budget 45 to 60 minutes, then add buffer anyway.

The tea ceremony (敬茶)

This is the heart of the customary wedding. The couple serves sweet tea to elders in order of seniority. Grandparents, then parents, then uncles and aunts, then older siblings. Each person is addressed by the right kinship title. Elders give ang bao or jewellery in return. It is often at the groom’s home after the fetching, then at the bride’s, or the other way around. Have one person on the teapot and the cups, and a cheat-sheet of titles. Do not make the couple be that person.

The banquet

Usually two march-ins, with a change of outfit. A short film, speeches, and the yam seng: three rounds, held as long as lungs allow. Then the couple toasts table to table. The hour-by-hour timeline shows how the morning and the banquet fit in one day.

Shortening things without a fight

  • Decide with both families, early, which customs you will keep, shorten or skip. The friction is almost always a surprise on the day, not the skipping itself.
  • Most of it compresses. Two tea ceremonies can become one sitting. Guo Da Li can be symbolic rather than lavish.
  • Write the plan down and share it, so both families are reading the same script.

Customs involve a lot of people. Write down who does what.
